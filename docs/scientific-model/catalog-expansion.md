# Catalog Expansion Model

## Purpose

Venom Atlas is organized across two independent dimensions:

1. Taxonomic breadth: which branches of life produce or carry biologically active toxins.
2. Exposure strategy: how a toxin moves from its source to another organism.

Taxonomy alone is not enough. A frog and a snake are both vertebrates, but passive skin poison and fang-injected venom require different data, language, safety context, and visualizations.

## Starter Reference Set

| Organism | Lineage | Strategy | Primary route | Framework pressure tested |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Red imported fire ant (*Solenopsis invicta*) | Insect | Venomous | Sting | Social defense, repeated stings, mixed venom |
| Golden poison frog (*Phyllobates terribilis*) | Amphibian | Poisonous | Contact or ingestion | Passive exposure, diet-dependent alkaloids |
| Inland taipan (*Oxyuranus microlepidotus*) | Reptile | Venomous | Bite | Fang injection, prey capture, neurotoxic syndrome |
| Reef stonefish (*Synanceia verrucosa*) | Ray-finned fish | Venomous | Spine | Pressure-triggered envenomation, aquatic exposure |
| Platypus (*Ornithorhynchus anatinus*) | Mammal | Venomous | Spur | Sex- and season-linked venom apparatus |

A starter profile establishes identity, taxonomy, habitat, toxic strategy, delivery route, and a public institutional source. It does not claim complete geography, chemistry, physiology, or media coverage.

## Exposure Strategies

Every organism must declare one of these strategies:

- `venomous`: actively delivers a toxic secretion using specialized anatomy.
- `poisonous`: causes exposure when toxins are touched, inhaled, or ingested.
- `both`: has independently supported active and passive toxic exposure modes.

Primary routes currently supported are sting, bite, spine, spur, contact, ingestion, inhalation, and unknown. New routes should be added only when the anatomical mechanism cannot be represented accurately by an existing route.

## Remaining Animal Categories

### Venomous invertebrates

- Cnidarians: box jellyfish, sea anemones, hydroids, and corals using nematocysts.
- Arachnids: spiders, scorpions, pseudoscorpions, and venomous ticks or mites where supported.
- Myriapods: centipedes using forcipules.
- Other insects: bees, wasps, parasitoid wasps, venomous caterpillars, and true bugs.
- Molluscs: cone snails, blue-ringed octopuses, and venomous cephalopods or gastropods.
- Annelids: blood-feeding and predatory worms with bioactive secretions.
- Echinoderms: venomous sea stars, sea urchins, and crown-of-thorns spines.
- Crustaceans: remipedes and any future clades with well-supported venom systems.

### Venomous vertebrates

- Reptiles: elapid, viperid, colubrid, and helodermatid delivery systems.
- Fishes: dorsal-spine, opercular-spine, and fang-like delivery systems.
- Amphibians: rare actively delivered secretions, kept distinct from poisonous skin defenses.
- Mammals: monotremes, solenodons, shrews, vampire bats, and slow lorises where venom status is supported.

### Poisonous animals

- Amphibians: poison frogs, newts, toads, and salamanders.
- Fishes: pufferfishes and other toxin-bearing species acquired through diet or symbionts.
- Birds: toxin-bearing pitohuis, ifrits, and other documented examples.
- Arthropods: toxin-sequestering beetles, moths, butterflies, and caterpillars.
- Marine invertebrates: species that accumulate toxins without an injection apparatus.

## Non-Animal Categories

These belong in the long-term atlas, but need contracts beyond an animal `deliveryMechanism`:

- Plants: contact toxins, ingested phytotoxins, phototoxins, and aerosolized irritants.
- Fungi: poisonous mushrooms, mycotoxin-producing molds, and exposure syndromes.
- Bacteria: secreted exotoxins and organism-associated toxin systems.
- Cyanobacteria and algae: bloom-associated cyanotoxins and phycotoxins.
- Dinoflagellates and other protists: food-web accumulation and aerosol exposure.
- Lichens: toxic secondary metabolites where ecological and clinical evidence exists.

Before adding these groups, introduce a producer/source model that does not assume an animal body, venom apparatus, or organism-to-human encounter.

## Cross-Cutting Categories

Each future record should also be classifiable by:

- Ecological function: predation, defense, competition, digestion, parasitism, or unknown.
- Molecular class: small molecule, peptide, protein, enzyme, glycoside, alkaloid, or complex mixture.
- Acquisition: synthesized, diet-sequestered, symbiont-derived, environmentally accumulated, or unknown.
- Target system: neural, cardiovascular, hemostatic, muscular, cytotoxic, renal, hepatic, immune, or mixed.
- Evidence context: in vitro, animal model, clinical observation, epidemiology, review, or database.
- Human exposure setting: occupational, foodborne, recreational, domestic, medical, or wildlife handling.

## Expansion Rule

Add breadth in cohorts that each challenge a new modeling assumption. Do not add five nearly identical snake profiles while plants, marine toxins, passive poisons, and microbial producers remain structurally impossible. Every cohort must leave the shared schema and validation stricter than it found them.
